Abstract
This project proposes an AI decision layer that gives ambulances a predictive, safety-validated green
corridor across a whole route, instead of one intersection at a time. Ambulances lose critical minutes at
congested signalised intersections, and green corridors are still largely arranged by hand.
Given an emergency request, the system selects a traffic-aware route to the most suitable hospital,
predicts when the ambulance will reach each intersection, and coordinates a moving green corridor,
restoring normal signal operation behind the vehicle. Development and evaluation run in a SUMO-based
digital twin of a Lahore corridor. The architecture is designed to plug into the live data and signal
infrastructure of the Punjab Safe Cities Authority (PSCA) wherever access is later authorised; no PSCA
collaboration is confirmed at this stage (Section 14).

2. Problem Statement
Ambulances in Lahore cross many congested signalised intersections with no automated, route-wide
priority, and every minute of delay reduces a patient's chance of a good outcome.
2.1 Background
Lahore is Pakistan's second-largest city and carries dense, mixed traffic: cars, motorcycles, rickshaws,
buses and trucks share the same signalised corridors, and peak-hour congestion is routine. An ambulance
responding to a cardiac arrest, stroke or major trauma must cross many of these intersections.

In practice, ambulances rely on sirens, the driver's judgement and occasionally manual coordination with
traffic staff. A green corridor, when arranged at all, is normally set up by radio or phone, covers a handful
of intersections and depends on human operators.
The Punjab Police Integrated Command, Control and Communication Centre (PPIC3) in Lahore already
brings together intelligent traffic management, a large CCTV network, centralised signal control (PTSC),
GPS-based dispatch (AVLS) and 1122 emergency coordination. What public documentation does not show
is an automated decision layer that uses these to serve a specific emergency vehicle.
2.2 The gaps
•
Reactive, not predictive. Priority is triggered only when the ambulance is close to a signal, leaving
no time to clear the queue already formed.
•
One intersection at a time. Existing preemption acts locally; nothing plans arrival time at every
intersection along the route.
•
Shortest distance is not fastest time. Routes ignore predicted congestion, signal delay and
incidents.
•
Manual and hard to scale. Operators cannot hand-configure corridors for several simultaneous
emergencies.
•
Side-effects are ignored. Turning every signal green causes cross-traffic gridlock and long recovery
times.
•
No safe place to experiment. Live signals cannot be a test bench, so new strategies are hard to
evaluate before deployment.
2.3 Formal problem statement
There is no automated, predictive and safety-validated decision layer that, for a given emergency vehicle,
(i) selects a traffic-aware route to the most suitable hospital, (ii) forecasts when the vehicle will reach each
intersection on that route, and (iii) coordinates signals across the entire route, while limiting delay to
normal traffic and preventing unsafe signal states.
Our contribution is emergency-specific, predictive, multi-intersection coordination, not "AI traffic
management" in general. Verifying what PSCA's ITMS already provides is the first task of the project
(Section 12).
3. Proposed Solution
We propose an AI emergency-priority decision layer built on top of existing traffic and
emergency-response infrastructure. It does not replace cameras, ITMS or signal controllers: it consumes
their data, decides what should happen along an ambulance's route, validates every action for safety, and
issues the resulting signal plan.
3.1 How the system works
When an emergency request arrives, the following pipeline runs automatically, without an operator
opening signals one by one:
1
Emergency request received – incident location, priority and assigned ambulance.
2
Traffic state acquired – vehicle count, speed, density and congestion level from CCTV / ITMS data.
3
Ambulance located – GPS position, speed and heading from AVLS, map-matched to the road
network.
4
Hospital selected – the most suitable destination by predicted travel time (and capacity or speciality
data, if available).
5
Route computed – traffic-aware A* using predicted travel time, signal delay and incident penalties
rather than distance.
6
Arrival predicted – estimated arrival at the destination and at every upcoming intersection.
7
Intersection traffic forecast – expected queues and flow at each intersection when the ambulance
will arrive.

8
Green corridor planned – which signals, when to start preparing, and how long to hold priority.
9
Safety validation – every command is checked for conflicts, minimum green, pedestrian phases and
cross-traffic impact.
10 Priority executed – commands are applied in SUMO and, only where PSCA authorises, requested
through the PTSC interface.
11 Progress monitored – route and corridor are re-planned if traffic changes (for example an accident
ahead).
12 Normal operation restored – each intersection returns to its regular plan as soon as the ambulance
has cleared it.
3.2 What makes the solution intelligent
•
Predictive control. Signals are prepared while the ambulance is still, say, 40 seconds away, so
queues clear before it arrives.
•
Route-wide coordination. One plan covers all intersections on the route and moves with the vehicle.
•
Traffic-aware routing. Edge costs use predicted travel time, congestion and incident penalties, so
the chosen route can differ from the shortest one.
•
Multi-objective decisions. The controller minimises ambulance delay while limiting extra delay,
queue growth and signal switching for normal traffic.
•
Dynamic re-routing. If congestion or an incident changes the best route, route and corridor are
recomputed on the fly.
•
Safe by design. AI components only recommend; a rule-based safety layer approves or rejects every
signal command.

4.3 Why our project is different
1
Predictive, not reactive. We forecast ambulance arrival and intersection traffic and prepare signals
ahead of time.
2
Routing and signals in one loop. Hospital and route selection, corridor planning and signal control
share the same digital twin and predictions.
3
Aware of side-effects. The objective penalises delay to normal traffic, queue growth and signal
switching, so the corridor does not create gridlock elsewhere.
4
Safety layer between AI and signals. Machine-learning and LLM components never command a
signal directly; a rule-based validator does.
5
Built for PSCA's ecosystem. It reuses existing data (CCTV analytics, AVLS, PTSC) instead of new
roadside hardware, and is designed for Lahore's mixed traffic, including motorcycles and rickshaws.
6
Measurable and reproducible. A calibrated SUMO digital twin lets us compare six control strategies
on the same scenarios with quantitative metrics.

8.2 Core modules
A. Perception and traffic-state estimation. A pretrained YOLO detector is fine-tuned on approved or
recorded footage; ByteTrack or BoT-SORT keeps vehicle IDs across frames; homography converts pixel
movement into metres. If only processed detections are available, the same downstream modules
consume them directly.
B. Prediction. Models grow in complexity: linear and random-forest baselines, then XGBoost / LightGBM,
then LSTM / GRU where enough time-series data exists. Targets are ambulance ETA, volume, speed, travel
time and congestion level.
C. Routing and hospital selection. A directed road graph whose edge cost is predicted travel time plus
congestion, incident and signal-delay penalties. Dijkstra and A* come first, then a time-dependent
traffic-weighted A*.
D. Green-corridor engine. Once a route is fixed, the engine calculates expected arrival at every
intersection and schedules signal actions ahead of it, following emergency vehicle preemption (EVP)
principles.
Intersection
Ambulance ETA
Action
I1
35 s
Green priority now; hold until the ambulance has cleared.
I2
70 s
Prepare: predict queue, shorten cross-street phase, clear approach.
I3
110 s

I4
155 s
Monitor and reserve a green slot.
As the ambulance passes I1 it returns to normal operation, I2 goes green, and I3 and I4 move to
preparation: a corridor that travels with the vehicle.
E. Signal optimisation and reinforcement learning. OR-Tools constrained optimisation is the first
controller. A PPO agent trained in a Gymnasium environment around SUMO (via libsumo) is then
investigated.
Element
Design
State
Lane densities and queues, current phase and duration, ambulance distance and ETA, downstream
density.
Actions
Keep phase, change phase, extend green, apply emergency priority (final set follows the actual signal
configuration).
Reward
Negative ambulance delay, normal-traffic delay, queue length and unnecessary phase switching; positive
emergency progress. A reward that only rewards ambulance movement is rejected because it can ruin all
other traffic.
F. Safety and validation layer. Every command is checked for valid phase, minimum green and
maximum red, no conflicting greens, protected pedestrian phases, cross-traffic impact within limits and
permitted emergency override. Rejected commands are logged; in live use, actions would also require
operator authorisation.

12. Data Access, Security and Compliance
If PSCA collaboration is confirmed, the team will first produce a technical requirements document that
settles what is available and permitted. Until then, the project proceeds with public, historical or synthetic
data.
The requirements document will settle:
•
What traffic, camera, signal, GIS and emergency data are available.
•
Whether raw CCTV or processed detections are provided, and whether historical data can be used.
•
What GPU, software and network resources exist on PSCA systems.
•
Whether external APIs and AI coding tools are permitted.
•
Whether models or code may leave the PSCA environment.
•
What level of signal-system integration is authorised.
Security principles:
•
All work involving PSCA data is carried out on PSCA-provided systems, in line with PSCA's data-security
policy; no data is copied to personal devices.
•
Sensitive data (camera frames, internal network information, raw surveillance data) is processed
locally and never sent to external services.
•
Emergency vehicles are identified by anonymous IDs; only location, speed, heading, timestamp and
destination are needed.
•
The system is offline-first, uses role-based access (Admin, PSCA Operator, Traffic Operator, Research
read-only) and keeps audit logs.
•
AI is advisory: all commands pass a safety validator and, in live use, operator authorisation.
13. Cloud Platform and Deployment Approach
The project does not plan to use a public cloud platform; it plans an on-premise, offline-first deployment
on Linux (Ubuntu). This is a tentative design choice and is subject to PSCA requirements and to university
and advisor guidance.
•
Why on-premise. PSCA's environment is expected to be restricted and offline-first, and sensitive
camera and traffic data must not leave local systems. Cloud-only services are therefore treated as
incompatible with the target environment.
•
What runs locally. Python services (FastAPI), PostgreSQL + PostGIS, Redis, SUMO, model training and
inference, and the React dashboard served as static files behind Nginx with TLS.

16. Expected Outcomes and Deliverables
The target result is a measurable reduction in ambulance travel and waiting time versus the baselines,
without disproportionate delay to normal traffic, in a system ready to connect to PSCA's infrastructure if
and when authorised.
•
A calibrated SUMO digital twin of a Lahore corridor.
•
A traffic-analytics pipeline (detection, tracking, speed, density, congestion).
•
Trained ETA and traffic-prediction models with evaluation results.
•
Traffic-aware routing with dynamic re-routing, and a predictive multi-intersection green-corridor engine
with a safety layer.
•
An RL controller, a constrained agentic coordination layer and a React operator dashboard.
•
A comparative evaluation of six strategies, a final report / thesis and a live demonstration.
Summary
Traffic and ambulance data → digital twin → AI prediction → dynamic route optimisation → green-corridor
decision → safety validation → traffic-signal coordination. The system is proven first in SUMO and connects
to PSCA's infrastructure only where access is authorised.
